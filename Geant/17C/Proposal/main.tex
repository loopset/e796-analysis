\begin{abstract}
    \noindent We propose to measure the $\nu sd$ strength in \iso{18}{C} through the stripping \iso{17}{C}(d,p)\iso{18}{C} transfer reaction to determine the size of the $N=16$ shell closure as the neutron drip line is approached in carbon isotopes. These nuclei provide an excellent basis for assessing the impact of three-nucleon forces and coupling-to-the-continuum effects, and we expect our results to extend previous observations in \iso{16,17}{C}. The experiment will also investigate possible negative-parity states in \iso{18}{C} relevant to the astrophysically important \iso{17}{C}(n,$\gamma$) reaction rate. We intend to use a high-purity \iso{17}{C} beam produced by the LISE spectrometer and directed onto the active-target ACTAR TPC, whose recent developments have demonstrated its capabilities for measuring (d,p) transfer reactions.
    % 
    % Neutron-rich carbon and oxygen isotopes are prominent examples of nuclei strongly affected by shell-structure migration driven by the residual $NN$ interaction. Several experiments have highlighted the erosion of the $N=20$ closure into the new $N=14$ and $N=16$ closures, caused by the changing tensor force between the $\pi psd$ and $\nu psd$ shells \cite{otsuka2020}. The carbon isotopic chain exhibits many exotic properties arising from this rearrangement of shell gaps; for example, the large deformation lengths of \iso{16}{C} \cite{elekes2004} and \iso{17}{C} \cite{elekes2005}, and the halo character of \iso{19}{C} \cite{nakamura1999} and \iso{22}{C} \cite{tanaka2010}. The $N=16$ gap, determined between the $\nu1s_{1/2}$ and $\nu0d_{3/2}$ orbitals, is essential for establishing the properties of the most neutron-rich carbon isotopes. While studying this gap towards the drip line remains challenging for current facilities, mapping its evolution in less exotic carbon isotopes is feasible. Benchmarking and improving advanced shell-model interactions in these systems is therefore essential to extend their predictive power to the most exotic nuclei. In this sense, \iso{18}{C} is an excellent test bench for which experimental knowledge of \textit{sd} strength remains limited. We therefore propose to study its single-particle structure via the neutron-adding transfer reaction \iso{17}{C}(d,p)\iso{18}{C}, using the \iso{17}{C} beam currently available at GANIL. This work follows the path of previous investigations, also at GANIL, of the \iso{16}{C}(d,p)\iso{17}{C} reaction \cite{pereira-lopez2020,lois-fuentes2025}.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Scientific motivation}
Neutron-rich carbon and oxygen isotopes are prominent examples of shell-structure migration driven by the residual $NN$ interaction. For oxygen isotopes, as neutrons are added to the $0d1s$ shell, the monopole $NN$ force, through the changing tensor force between the $\pi psd$ and $\nu psd$ shells \cite{otsuka2020}, causes the breakdown of the $N=20$ magic number into the new $N=14$ ($0d_{5/2} - 1s_{1/2}$) and $N=16$ ($1s_{1/2} - 0d_{3/2}$) shell closures, making \iso{22}{O} and the drip-line nucleus \iso{24}{O} doubly magic. When moving from oxygen to carbon isotopes, two protons are removed from the $\pi0p_{1/2}$ orbital, leaving it empty. This causes a major reorganisation of the neutron orbitals, making the $N=14$ closure vanish for carbon \cite{stanoiu2008}. The $N=16$ gap should remain large enough to bind the last neutron-rich carbon isotope, the two-neutron halo \iso{22}{C}. However, precisely determining the size of this gap towards the drip line remains a challenge for present RIB facilities. Instead, it is feasible to infer its size by extrapolating measurements in less demanding neutron-rich carbon isotopes. Following a recent investigation of the $N=16$ closure in \iso{17}{C} \cite{lois-fuentes2025}, we propose to extend the systematic trends by measuring it directly via the \iso{17}{C}(d,p)\iso{18}{C} transfer reaction profiting from the high-quality \iso{17}{C} beam provided by the LISE spectrometer at GANIL.

On the one hand, measuring shell closures in neutron-rich nuclei offers stringent tests of shell-model (SM) calculations using modern effective interactions. The previous results on \iso{17}{C} have shown that one such interaction, SFO-tls, still fails to reproduce the evolution of the $0d_{3/2}$ effective single-particle energy (ESPE) in carbon isotopes, requiring phenomenological corrections to the underlying $NN$ forces to reproduce the experimental data. This is shown in the left panel of \autoref{fig:exp_17c}, where the $0d_{3/2}$ ESPE in \iso{16}{C} (inferred from the \iso{17}{C} measurement) is larger than the SM predictions by \qty{1.3}{\MeV}. Our proposed measurement in \iso{18}{C} will determine the $N=16$ closure by simultaneously measuring the excitation energy (\ex) and the spectroscopic factors (\ctwos) for final states.

On the other hand, for carbon isotopes starting at \iso{15}{C}, the $0d_{3/2}$ strength lies above the neutron-emission threshold, offering an excellent basis for studying effects often not captured by the shell model, namely coupling to the continuum and three-nucleon ($3N$) forces. Continuum effects were also tested in the previous \iso{16}{C}(d,p)\iso{17}{C} experiment, in which a Gamow shell model (GSM) calculation explicitly accounting for coupling to the continuum provided a good reproduction of the distribution of populated states \cite{lois-fuentes2025}.

Electromagnetic observables, such as reduced transition probabilities $B(\sigma\lambda)$ and branching ratios, have also underscored the important role of the continuum and three-nucleon forces, as inferred from \textit{ab initio} calculations. Lifetime measurements of the $2^+_1$ state in \iso{18}{C} \cite{voss2012} found a marked suppression of the reduced $B(E2)$ transition strength despite a low excitation energy ($\ex(2^+_1)=\qty{1.6}{\MeV}$), a notable anomaly because the $B(E2)$ and $\ex(2^+_1)$ observables are expected to be inversely related. A comparison with the \textit{ab initio} no-core shell model (NCSM), with and without $3N$ forces, revealed their importance in reproducing the experimental observables. Similar conclusions were drawn from analyses of the $2^+_2\rightarrow2^+_1$ branching ratios in \iso{16}{C} \cite{petri2012} and the $B(M1)$ strengths in \iso{17}{C} \cite{smalley2015}. The latter results are shown in the right panel of \autoref{fig:exp_17c}, where a phenomenological NCSM calculation that reproduces the binding energies of the lowest-lying states of \iso{17}{C}, explicitly accounts for the continuum, and includes $3N$ forces, provides a better description of the experimental results (the two rightmost columns in the figure).

In the present experiment, we will determine the spectroscopic factors for transfers between $\iso{17}{C}(3/2^+)_{\text{g.s.}}$ and several final states in $\iso{18}{C}(J^{\pi})$. These factors, which represent the radial overlap of the nuclear wave functions between the initial and final states, are extracted directly from the observed angular distributions. As such, they are extremely sensitive to the nuclear theory used to evaluate the wave functions and can be used to constrain the effects of $3N$ forces and other beyond-SM mechanisms. The proposed experiment will therefore provide much higher sensitivity to these underlying phenomena than previous electromagnetic observations.

Apart from serving as a benchmark for shell-model and \textit{ab initio} calculations, \iso{18}{C} is a semi-waiting point in the astrophysical \textit{r}-process, and its structure influences the \iso{17}{C}(n,$\gamma$) reaction rate that determines the abundances of actinides in core-collapse supernova explosions \cite{sasaqui2005}. In this carbon isotope, the one-neutron ($\sn = \qty{4.2}{\MeV}$) and two-neutron ($\stwon = \qty{4.9}{\MeV}$) thresholds lie only \qty{0.7}{\MeV} apart, and any resonance present in this region could affect the \textit{r}-process path. Since the standard shell model does not describe near-threshold effects well, a three-body model may be better suited for this energy region. The model of \cite{yakhelef2010}, shown in \autoref{fig:scheme_18c}, predicts resonances in this region, the most important being a $1^-$ $p$-wave resonance at \qty{5.1}{\MeV} with an estimated width of \qty{0.15}{\MeV} and a significant $E1$ strength. The search for such a resonance is therefore also within the scope of this experiment, since any $l=1$ state in the unbound region could have astrophysical impact.

% In \iso{18}{C}, the one-neutron ($\sn = \qty{4.2}{\MeV}$) and two-neutron ($\stwon = \qty{4.9}{\MeV}$) thresholds lie only \qty{0.7}{\MeV} apart. This part of the energy spectrum dominates the rate of the \iso{17}{C}(n,$\gamma$)\iso{18}{C} reaction, which determines the abundances of actinides in core-collapse supernovae. Studies performed with a three-body model \cite{yakhelef2010} have predicted several states in this astrophysically important region, the most important being a $1^-$ $p$-wave resonance at \qty{5.1}{\MeV} with an estimated width of \qty{150}{\keV}. The search for this state is therefore a key objective, since a large measured $E1$ strength would directly affect the final \textit{r}-process abundances.

Finally, recent attention has focused on Efimov physics in carbon isotopes \cite{amorim1997}, particularly the two-neutron halo $\iso{20}{C} = \iso{18}{C} + \iso{}{n} + \iso{}{n}$. In these systems, none of the two-body subsystems is bound, but they exhibit a large \textit{s}-wave scattering length. This produces an important long-range three-body interaction, leading to characteristic bound states close to the particle-emission threshold (\sn here). Although no conclusive experimental results for \iso{20}{C} are available, a more complete spectroscopic knowledge of \iso{18}{C} is essential to constrain the core structure in the three-body calculation more tightly.

\subsection{Available data on \texorpdfstring{\iso{18}{C}}{18C}}
The present spectroscopic knowledge of \iso{18}{C} is insufficient to address the questions raised in the previous paragraphs. Spectroscopy of \iso{18}{C} is limited to in-beam $\gamma$ spectroscopy \cite{stanoiu2008}, lifetime measurements \cite{ong2008,voss2012}, one-neutron knockout from \iso{19}{C} \cite{kondo2009}, and Coulomb dissociation of \iso{18,19}{C} \cite{heine2017}. From these experiments, only the \ctwos linking the ground states of both \iso{17}{C} and \iso{18}{C} was determined; it was found to agree with shell-model calculations using the WBP interaction, which predict a rather low $\ctwos = \qty{0.10}{}$ for the removal of $\nu0d_{3/2}$. For the excited states, no \ctwos are available. Apart from the lower-lying $2^+$ states, no clear spin-parity assignments exist for the remaining states. Our stripping reaction will help determine their $J^\pi$ through the characteristic $l$ shape of the transfer cross sections.

In light of \autoref{fig:scheme_18c}, the standard shell model (here using the WBT interaction) differs from the experimental level scheme by \qtyrange{1}{2}{\MeV}; however, it provides a clear and direct way to estimate the dominant $l$ contributions to each \iso{18}{C} state. Finally, the figure includes predictions from the CCEI approach using $3N$ forces from chiral effective theory \cite{jansen2014}. The two lower-lying $2^+$ states are in fairly good agreement with the experimental data, although more data are required to assess the higher part of the spectrum.

In particular, this experiment will allow us to test whether the trend observed in \iso{16}{C}(d,p)\iso{17}{C} \cite{pereira-lopez2020} of negligible $\nu0d_{3/2}$ strength in low-lying states\footnote{In \iso{17}{C}, the $3/2^+$ ground state has a $\iso{16}{C}(2^+)\otimes\nu0d_{5/2}$ configuration} continues in \iso{18}{C}, as predicted by the shell-model calculations in \autoref{tab:sm}. Measuring the $2^+_1$ state will then locate a major fragment of the $\nu0d_{5/2}$ orbital, whereas the $2^+_2$ state will carry a significant fraction of the $1s_{1/2}$ orbital. Apart from constraining $3N$ forces, locating these two orbitals in \iso{18}{C} will help confirm the disappearance of the $N=14$ shell gap \cite{stanoiu2008}.

In the unbound region, despite important phase-space contributions, our experiment will reach a sensitivity as low as $\ctwos = \qty{0.04}{}$\footnote{Reached, for instance, in the \iso{20}{O}(d,t) experiment \cite{lozano-gonzalez2026a}}, enabling observation of the astrophysically important $1^-$ state, depending on its spectroscopic factor. Around \qty{8}{\MeV}, the $\nu0d_{3/2}$ strength is expected to appear, maintaining a considerable $N=16$ gap. Based on recent transfer-experiment results \cite{loisfuentes2026,lozano-gonzalez2026a} obtained with ACTAR TPC, and with a setup optimised to extend the \ex coverage (see next section), we believe we can cover the $\ex \leq \qty{15}{\MeV}$ region and reconstruct a major fraction of the $\nu0d_{3/2}$ strength. By combining the $\nu1s_{1/2}$ and $\nu0d_{3/2}$ strengths, the energy centroids of both orbitals can be determined and the $N=16$ closure estimated. These results will provide a stringent test of the shell model and call for a better treatment of the continuum and $3N$ forces.

The experiment will use a LISE \iso{17}{C} beam slowed to \qty{15}{A\MeV} and sent into the active-target ACTAR TPC, where the reactions will occur and the light particles will be detected. \subsection{Beam production}
The \iso{17}{C} beam will be produced by fragmentation of a primary \iso{22}{Ne} beam at \qty{60}{\MeV\per\atomicmassunit} in a Be primary target. It will then be slowed to the final beam energy of \qty{15}{A\MeV}, selected and purified using a \qty{2}{\mm}-thick Be achromatic degrader placed in the LISE3 spectrometer. The resulting secondary beam is expected to be \qty{100}{\percent} pure, with an intensity of \qty{1e3}{pps}, and present an energy spread of \qty{2.5}{\percent}, according to the best settings of the spectrometer.

\subsection{Light-particle detection with ACTAR TPC}
The active target ACTAR TPC will be used as both the reaction target and the light-particle detector. The proposed configuration is based on the successful setup of the \iso{11}{Li}(d,p)\iso{12}{Li} experiment carried out at TRIUMF in 2025. In this configuration, ACTAR will be filled with a mixture of \qty{95}{\percent} D$_2$ and \qty{5}{\percent} CF$_4$ at \qty{900}{\milli\bar}, equivalent to a solid CD$_2$ target of \qty{12}{\milli\g\per\cm\squared}. As in previous transfer experiments \cite{loisfuentes2026}, auxiliary Si detector walls will be placed on both lateral and frontal flanges to stop and measure the residual energies of protons escaping the active-area region. However, because the (d,p) cross sections peak at large laboratory angles (or equivalently, at low $\theta_{\text{CM}}$; see \autoref{fig:simu_kin_ex}), where protons have low energies, the most relevant part of the angular distribution will be measured by detecting protons stopped within the pad-plane region. This requires use of the internal multiplicity trigger of ACTAR (the \textit{L1} trigger), which was also commissioned during the TRIUMF campaign \cite{blanco-calvino-thesis}. In this way, the proposed experiment will exploit the full kinematic capabilities recently achieved with ACTAR TPC.

Detailed simulations accounting for detector geometry, resolution, straggling, and beam emittance were carried out to determine the optimal detector configuration and expected yields. The results are shown in \autoref{fig:simu_kin_ex}, where the simulated kinematic lines and excitation-energy (\ex) spectrum for the \iso{17}{C}(d,p)\iso{18}{C} reaction are displayed for selected \ex states: ground state, the two low-lying $2^+$ states, the predicted $1^-$ state ($\Gamma = \qty{0.15}{\MeV}$), and three hypothetical $l=0$ (from adding to $0d_{3/2}$) at \qty{6.4}{}, \qty{10}{} and \qty{15}{\MeV} (with $\Gamma = \qtylist{0.5;1;2}{\MeV}$, respectively). The characteristic (d,p) shape of the kinematic lines, in which higher-\ex states correspond to lower proton laboratory energies, makes the \textit{L1} trigger essential for accessing the $\ex > \sn$ range. The inset in \autoref{fig:simu_kin_ex} shows the evolution of the experimental resolution with excitation energy, yielding an overall constant resolution of $\sigma \simeq \qty{250}{\keV}$.

The left panel of \autoref{fig:eff_theoxs} presents the calculated efficiencies for representative \ex states in \iso{18}{C}. As noted above, the \textit{L1} trigger will be essential for accessing the low-angle part of the angular distribution, even though its efficiency depends strongly on the beam emittance (here assumed to be similar to that of the previous \iso{20}{O} experiment) and on the trigger exclusion zone. In this proposal, a conservative exclusion region of twelve rows above and below the central row was assumed. These settings could be adjusted during the experiment to maximise the cross-section coverage while avoiding triggering on beam particles with large emittance. The right panel of the same figure displays the theoretical angular distributions for $l=0, 1, \text{and}\, 2$ transfer calculated within the ADWA theory using the TWOFNR code. These were included in the generation of random $\theta_{\text{CM}}$ events to make the simulation as realistic as possible.

Regarding the Si detector setup, this experiment uses the newly deployed \qty{1.5}{\mm}-thick Si modules on both left and right walls to enlarge the $\theta_{\text{CM}}$ coverage by increasing the range of proton energies stopped in the Si material. Bearing in mind the availability of just 18 modules (each with two Si pads), each lateral wall will use 8 modules disposed in two columns and four rows. The front wall will use the legacy Si detector setup, composed of four columns and four rows of \qty{0.5}{\mm}-thick Si pads, resembling the \iso{20}{O}(d,\iso{3}{He})\iso{19}{N} experiment configuration \cite{loisfuentes2026} and enabling also the simultaneous study of the interesting \iso{17}{C}(d,t)\iso{16}{C} reaction.

All Si walls will be placed as close as possible to the pad plane, with typical distances of \qtyrange{5}{7}{\cm}, while ensuring minimum disturbance to the drift electric field. In light of \autoref{fig:eff_theoxs}, the combined Si and pad-plane coverage will allow measuring $\theta_{\text{CM}} \leq \qty{60}{\degree}$ angles, with an average efficiency of \qty{20}{\percent} across the full range, and reaching \ex above \qty{10}{\MeV}. These estimates ensure a correct distinction of the different characteristic $l$ shapes of the angular distributions, as shown in \autoref{fig:rec_xs}.

Finally, particle identification will be achieved through standard methods within the ACTAR TPC collaboration. Protons stopped within the active area will be identified in a 2D matrix containing the total collected charge ($\propto \Delta$E) against the particle range ($\propto\,E$), a method that showed good separation capabilities between protons and deuterons in the ongoing analysis of the TRIUMF campaign \cite{blanco-calvino-thesis}. For particles reaching the Si walls, light recoils are selected by considering the collected charge averaged over the track length ($\propto \Delta$E) against the residual energy in the Si detectors, a method that ensured clear PID in previous experiments \cite{loisfuentes2026,lozano-gonzalez2026a}.

\subsection{Heavy-particle detection in the ZDD}
\autoref{fig:simu_kin_ex} (right) shows that both the one-neutron and two-neutron emission thresholds in \iso{18}{C} are close in energy, at around \qty{4.5}{\MeV}. As a result, an important part of the $d$ strength lies in the unbound region, with intrinsic widths $\Gamma$ in the \qtyrange{0.1}{2.5}{\MeV} range. The study of their partial widths $\Gamma_{\text{1n}}$ and $\Gamma_{\text{2n}}$ would provide valuable information on each state's underlying structure. However, this requires identification of the heavy fragments in both mass $A$ and charge $Z$, which is not possible with ACTAR TPC alone. For this reason, we propose to couple ACTAR TPC with the recently developed zero-degree detector (ZDD) for LISE \cite{girard-alcindor2023}, enabling clear identification of the different heavy recoils. Recent experiments with the ZDD achieved only charge identification, owing to various problems. Here, we expect that the low mass of our ejectiles, together with the lower beam intensity, will allow us to use the E-$\Delta$E method to tag their mass as well. An example of the identification capabilities of the ZDD is shown in \autoref{fig:zdd}.
\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/zdd.png}
    \caption{E-$\Delta$E identification matrix obtained after low-intensity \iso{50}{Cr} fragmentation at \qty{30}{\MeV\per\atomicmassunit}, taken from \cite{girard-alcindor2023}. A good separation in $(Z,A)$ is obtained for the different fragments.}
    \label{fig:zdd}
\end{figure}

The ZDD will be placed downstream of ACTAR TPC as close as possible to maximise the geometric efficiency, using a specifically designed flange. The recoiling \iso{18}{C} emerge with angles as larger as \qty{5}{\degree}, which will require leaving a hole of $r = \qty{4}{\cm}$ in the front Si wall so that they can reach the backward detector. Simulations are ongoing to determine the best arrangement of the ZDD in order to maximise the geometric efficiency.

\section*{Beam time request}
The theoretical cross sections shown in the left panel of \autoref{fig:eff_theoxs} have an average value of \qty{1}{\milli\barn\per\steradian}, assuming a $\ctwos = \qty{1}{}$. The yield can then be calculated from the formula $Y = I_{\text{17C}}\cdot N_{\text{d}}\cdot \epsilon \cdot \Omega \cdot \ctwos\cdot\text{d}\sigma/\text{d}\Omega$, where we assume an average beam intensity of $I_{\text{17C}} = \qty{1e3}{pps}$, a deuteron number from the gas mixture of $N_{\text{d}} = \qty{1.1e21}{\deuteron\per\cm\squared}$, a mean efficiency of $\epsilon = \qty{17}{\percent}$, a typical solid-angle interval at $\thetacm = \qty{25}{\degree}$ with a width of \qty{5}{\degree} ($\Omega = \qty{0.23}{\steradian}$), and a more realistic $\ctwos = \qty{0.25}{}$. This gives a yield of $Y = \qty{1.1e-5}{\counts\per\s}$, and therefore we request 21 UT (7 days) to reach statistical uncertainties of \qty{40}{\percent} in \qty{5}{\degree} bins across the covered $\thetacm$ range.
